Moving Drilling Rigs, High-Clearance Equipment/Loads and Crane Operations Near Power Lines

About GI 2.702

This page is a study and revision guide for Saudi Aramco requirements concerning the movement or operation of equipment and loads where overhead electrical power lines may create a hazard.

Important: This page is for study and reference purposes only. It does not replace the current Saudi Aramco General Instruction, approved work procedures, electrical clearances, site requirements, lift plans, permits, or competent-person assessment.

1. Why Power Lines Are a Major Lifting Hazard

Cranes, booms, loads, rigging equipment and high-clearance vehicles can come dangerously close to overhead electrical conductors. Electrical contact can result in serious injury, fatalities, equipment damage, fire and operational disruption.

Potential hazards

  • Direct contact between crane and conductor.
  • Electrical arcing without direct physical contact.
  • Energized crane components.
  • Electrical current passing through rigging or the load.
  • Shock to personnel touching the crane or load.
  • Unexpected boom or load movement.
  • Loss of control during transportation.

2. Equipment That Requires Special Attention

  • Mobile cranes.
  • Crawler cranes.
  • Truck-mounted cranes.
  • All-terrain cranes.
  • Rough-terrain cranes.
  • Knuckle-boom cranes.
  • Telescopic handlers.
  • High-load transport vehicles.
  • Drilling rigs.
  • Other high-clearance equipment.
The complete operating envelope must be considered, not only the crane's normal parked position. This includes boom movement, load movement, equipment travel and possible changes in configuration.

3. Planning Before Work Starts

Work near overhead electrical lines requires planning before equipment enters the area.

Planning considerations

  • Identify overhead power lines.
  • Determine the electrical hazards associated with the lines.
  • Identify the equipment operating envelope.
  • Consider maximum boom and load positions.
  • Consider transportation routes.
  • Identify potential contact points.
  • Establish required controls and exclusion areas.
  • Confirm required permits and approvals.
  • Brief all personnel involved.
  • Establish an emergency response plan.

4. Work Area Assessment

Before beginning the operation, the work area should be assessed for overhead electrical hazards and other site conditions.

Check for

  • Overhead transmission lines.
  • Distribution lines.
  • Temporary electrical installations.
  • Electrical substations.
  • Poles and supporting structures.
  • Restricted access areas.
  • Uneven ground.
  • Excavations and underground hazards.
  • Traffic and other moving equipment.

5. Crane Operating Envelope

A crane's operating envelope includes more than the physical position of the crane itself.

  • Crane base location.
  • Outrigger extension.
  • Boom length.
  • Boom angle.
  • Jib configuration.
  • Hook block position.
  • Load dimensions.
  • Load swing.
  • Wind effects.
  • Possible equipment movement.
The complete configuration and possible movement must be considered when evaluating electrical-line hazards.

6. High-Clearance Loads

High loads can create electrical hazards even when the transporting vehicle itself appears to have adequate clearance.

Examples

  • Long structural members.
  • Large vessels.
  • Pipe assemblies.
  • Drilling equipment.
  • Storage tanks.
  • Large machinery.
  • Fabricated structures.

Load dimensions should be confirmed before transportation or lifting. Changes in route, load orientation or equipment configuration can change the electrical clearance.

7. Drilling Rig Movement

Moving a drilling rig or other tall equipment requires particular attention to overhead obstructions and electrical conductors.

Study considerations

  • Confirm the travel route.
  • Identify all overhead electrical hazards.
  • Consider the highest point of the equipment.
  • Consider mast and derrick configuration.
  • Control movement speed.
  • Use competent personnel for guidance.
  • Maintain effective communication.
  • Stop if conditions become unsafe.

8. Spotter / Signaller

A designated competent person may be required to assist with equipment movement and maintain awareness of hazards.

Key responsibilities

  • Maintain a clear view of the hazard area where possible.
  • Communicate clearly with the operator.
  • Monitor equipment movement.
  • Warn the operator of developing hazards.
  • Stop the operation when unsafe conditions arise.
  • Avoid becoming positioned between the equipment and a hazard.
The spotter must not rely on guesswork for electrical clearance. Required clearances and controls must be established from the applicable current requirements and site-approved procedure.

9. Communication

Clear communication is essential when equipment operates near electrical hazards.

  • Use an agreed communication method.
  • Use recognized hand signals where applicable.
  • Use radio communication when appropriate.
  • Ensure the operator understands the signal.
  • Use one designated signaller where required.
  • Stop when communication is lost or unclear.

10. Electrical Isolation

Where work requires an electrical isolation, the isolation must be established through the authorized electrical and site process.

  • Do not assume that a line is de-energized.
  • Do not rely on visual appearance.
  • Follow the approved isolation procedure.
  • Use authorized personnel for electrical isolation.
  • Confirm the required controls before work begins.

11. Electrical Approach Controls

Specific minimum approach distances and controls depend on the applicable current standard, electrical system and site procedure.

Do not use a memorized clearance number from this study page as an operational limit. Always verify the current applicable Saudi Aramco requirement and approved site procedure before performing work.

Study the following concepts

  • Voltage level.
  • Required approach distance.
  • Equipment height.
  • Maximum boom elevation.
  • Load dimensions.
  • Potential load swing.
  • Wind and environmental effects.
  • Controlled access.

12. Barriers and Exclusion Zones

Physical and administrative controls can help prevent equipment from entering hazardous areas.

  • Use suitable barriers where required.
  • Mark restricted areas clearly.
  • Control vehicle access.
  • Control crane positioning.
  • Keep unauthorized personnel away.
  • Maintain the approved operating boundary.

13. Wind and Environmental Conditions

Environmental conditions can affect equipment and load movement.

  • Wind can cause load swing.
  • Wind can affect long loads.
  • Poor visibility can affect communication.
  • Dust can reduce visibility.
  • Weather can change during the operation.
If environmental conditions make the operation unsafe, the operation should be stopped and reassessed in accordance with the approved procedure.

14. Emergency Response

Electrical contact with a crane or other equipment can create a dangerous situation.

  • Warn personnel to stay clear.
  • Do not touch the crane and ground simultaneously.
  • Do not approach an energized crane.
  • Follow the site's emergency procedure.
  • Notify the appropriate emergency/site authority.
  • Do not resume work until the hazard has been made safe.
Emergency actions must follow the current site emergency procedure and direction from qualified electrical/emergency personnel.

15. Risk Assessment

A risk assessment should identify hazards, evaluate risk and establish suitable controls before work begins.

Typical sequence

Identify Hazard → Assess Risk → Select Controls → Communicate → Execute Under Control → Monitor → Stop/Reassess if Conditions Change

16. Lift Planning Near Power Lines

  • Confirm load weight.
  • Confirm load dimensions.
  • Confirm crane configuration.
  • Confirm boom and jib configuration.
  • Determine working radius.
  • Review load path.
  • Identify electrical hazards.
  • Establish required controls.
  • Confirm communication arrangements.
  • Conduct the pre-lift briefing.

17. Stop Work Conditions

Personnel should stop the operation when an unsafe condition develops or when the approved controls can no longer be maintained.

  • Loss of communication.
  • Unexpected equipment movement.
  • Loss of required clearance.
  • Unexpected load swing.
  • Changing weather conditions.
  • Unauthorized personnel entering the area.
  • Change in equipment configuration.
  • Change in the approved work scope.
  • Unidentified electrical hazard.

18. Rigger Study Points

  • Understand electrical hazards around lifting operations.
  • Understand the importance of the load path.
  • Recognize potential boom and load movement.
  • Maintain effective communication.
  • Follow the approved lift plan.
  • Use inspected lifting equipment.
  • Keep personnel outside hazardous zones.
  • Stop work when conditions become unsafe.

19. Operator Study Points

  • Know the crane configuration.
  • Understand the load chart.
  • Know the operating radius.
  • Understand the planned load path.
  • Maintain communication with the signaller.
  • Monitor surrounding hazards.
  • Do not proceed on unclear signals.
  • Stop when required controls cannot be maintained.
