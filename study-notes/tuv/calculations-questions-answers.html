Use this page to practise basic lifting calculations, understand formulas, and revise important rigging concepts.

Important safety notice: These are original educational practice examples, not official TÜV examination questions or an approved lifting plan. Actual lifting operations require verified load data, manufacturer ratings, applicable standards, the approved lift plan and competent-person verification.

1. Kilograms and tonnes

Tonnes = Kilograms ÷ 1,000

Worked example

A load weighs 2,500 kg. Convert it to tonnes.

Step 1: Divide 2,500 by 1,000.

Answer: 2.5 tonnes.
Q1. Convert 4,800 kg to tonnes.
4,800 ÷ 1,000 = 4.8 tonnes.
Q2. Convert 1.75 tonnes to kilograms.
1.75 × 1,000 = 1,750 kg.
Q3. A load weighs 6,250 kg. What is its weight in tonnes?
6,250 ÷ 1,000 = 6.25 tonnes.

2. Equal load sharing

Load per leg = Total load ÷ Number of equally loaded legs

This is a simplified calculation for idealised equal load sharing. It must not be assumed that every sling leg in a real arrangement shares the load equally.

Worked example

A 1,000 kg load is supported equally by two vertical sling legs.

1,000 ÷ 2 = 500 kg per leg.

Answer: 500 kg per leg under the stated assumptions.
Q4. A 1,600 kg load is equally supported by two vertical legs. What is the load per leg?
1,600 ÷ 2 = 800 kg per leg.
Q5. A 900 kg load is ideally shared equally by three vertical legs. What is the share per leg?
900 ÷ 3 = 300 kg per leg. This is only a mathematical example; actual load distribution must be established for the real arrangement.
Q6. Can you always divide the load by the number of sling legs?
No. Sling geometry, leg stiffness, centre of gravity and arrangement can produce unequal loading. Use the approved calculation and lift plan.

3. Sling tension and sling angle

For a simplified, symmetrical two-leg sling arrangement, with the angle measured from the horizontal:

Tension per leg = W ÷ (2 × sin θ)

W = supported load force in kN.

θ = sling angle measured from horizontal.

Worked example

The supported load force is 10 kN and both sling legs are at 60° from horizontal.

sin 60° ≈ 0.866

Tension = 10 ÷ (2 × 0.866)

Tension ≈ 5.77 kN per leg.

Answer: approximately 5.77 kN per leg, under the stated idealised assumptions.
Q7. What happens to sling tension when the angle from horizontal decreases?
Sling-leg tension generally increases as the angle becomes shallower. Horizontal forces on the load and lifting points can also increase.
Q8. Why must you confirm the angle reference before calculating sling tension?
The formula depends on whether the angle is measured from horizontal or vertical. Using the wrong reference can produce an incorrect result.
Q9. Is the formula above sufficient to approve a real lift?
No. It is a simplified educational model. The actual arrangement, load distribution, equipment ratings, lifting points, manufacturer instructions and approved plan must all be checked.

4. Percentage of rated capacity

Percentage used = (Applied load ÷ Rated capacity) × 100

Worked example

An illustrative load is 3 tonnes and the applicable rated capacity is 5 tonnes.

(3 ÷ 5) × 100 = 60%

Answer: 60% of the stated rated capacity. This percentage alone does not prove that a lift is safe.
Q10. A 2-tonne load is compared with a 4-tonne rated capacity. What percentage is used?
(2 ÷ 4) × 100 = 50%.
Q11. A load is rated at 4 tonnes, but the equipment capacity for the actual configuration is only 3 tonnes. Can the lift proceed?
No. The planned load exceeds the stated applicable capacity. Stop and have the lift arrangement reviewed and corrected before proceeding.

5. Load weight and additional equipment

The crane's applicable capacity calculation must account for the load and the relevant rigging, attachments and deductions according to the crane manufacturer's load-chart instructions.

Q12. Why is it important to know the actual load weight?
Load weight is essential for selecting suitable equipment and checking the planned lift against applicable ratings. Do not rely on a guess when reliable weight information is required.
Q13. Should rigging weight be ignored when checking a crane load chart?
No. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for the total suspended load and any required deductions.

6. Crane working radius and load charts

Working radius is generally the horizontal distance from the crane's relevant axis of rotation to the load line, as defined by the manufacturer.

Remember: Crane capacity cannot be determined from radius alone. Use the correct manufacturer's load chart for the actual crane configuration, setup and operating conditions.
Q14. Can crane capacity be determined from working radius alone?
No. The correct load chart and the crane's actual configuration and conditions must be verified.
Q15. Why can increasing the crane's working radius reduce its rated capacity?
Increasing radius generally increases the overturning moment. The permitted capacity depends on the specific crane and its manufacturer's load chart.

7. Centre of gravity and load balance

Q16. What is the centre of gravity (COG)?
It is the point through which the resultant weight of an object can be considered to act.
Q17. What may happen if a load is lifted from unsuitable lifting points?
The load may tilt, rotate, become unstable or overload equipment or lifting points.
Q18. What should you do if a load tilts unexpectedly during the initial test lift?
Stop the lift and lower the load safely if possible. Reassess the centre of gravity, lifting points and rigging arrangement before proceeding.

9. Final revision questions

Q19. What should happen if actual lifting conditions differ from the approved lift plan?
Stop and reassess the risks. Make any required changes through the approved process and obtain the necessary authorisation before continuing.
Q20. What should you do if a lifting accessory is damaged or its capacity cannot be verified?
Remove it from service, prevent further use and report it according to the site's procedure. Never guess its capacity or use damaged equipment.
